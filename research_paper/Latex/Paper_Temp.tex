\title{Do World Model Diagnostics Agree?\\ A Cross Diagnostic Validity Study on a Controlled Model Zoo}

\author{Venkatesh~Nagarjuna, Siddardha~Reddy, Nikhil~Obuleni, Viharika~Vemparala, and~Amir~Jafari%
\thanks{The authors are with the Data Science Program, The George Washington University, Washington, DC, USA.}}

\markboth{WM-Concord, Fall 2026}{Nagarjuna \MakeLowercase{\textit{et al.}}: Do World Model Diagnostics Agree?}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Four families of diagnostics are used to claim that a sequence model has, or lacks, a world model: next token
validity, state equivalence, inductive bias probes and representation probing. Each has been applied to different
models, so their conflicting verdicts cannot be compared. We run all four on one controlled zoo of 81 Othello models
(three architectures, three scales, three training distributions, three seeds) with a known ground truth, under an
analysis preregistered with git tags, and judge them against a fragility suite frozen before any score. Raw
agreement is strong (Kendall W 0.87), but with the legal move rate controlled W falls to 0.27, and no pair of the
diagnostics that can be separated from competence still agrees. The remaining disagreement is not explained by
architecture, scale or training data. Only the representation probe adds to the legal move rate in predicting
fragility, and this rests on three of 27 configurations: an exploratory analysis traces it to the models trained on
human games, which are both the most fragile and the least probe readable, and among comparable models the link
disappears. Whole game measures add two failures the next move unit misses: models trained on human games break
once play leaves human lines, and LSTMs lose track of the board over long games. A study that reports one diagnostic
on models of different quality therefore mostly reports how good the models are. The same pipeline is extended to
city navigation, where our code reproduces the published results of Vafa et al.
\end{abstract}

World models, interpretability, probing, measurement validity, Othello, preregistration.

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{W}{hether} a sequence model trained only to predict the next token builds a coherent internal
model of its domain is a central open question in machine learning. Several groups have built diagnostics to answer
it, but each uses its own instrument, on its own models, in its own domain. The same Othello models have been
reported both as having a world model~\cite{li2023othello, nanda2023emergent, yuan2025revisiting} and as lacking a
coherent one~\cite{vafa2024evaluating, vafa2025inductive}. Because the instruments and the models differ at the
same time, this disagreement cannot be interpreted: it may come from the diagnostics, from the models, or from
both.

We take the measurement view of Jacobs and Wallach~\cite{jacobs2021measurement}: ``has a world model'' is an
unobservable construct, and each diagnostic is one operationalization of it. Two questions then follow directly.
Do the operationalizations agree when they are applied to the same models (convergent validity)? And does any of
them predict an outcome it should predict, namely failure when the situation shifts but the rules do not
(predictive validity)? We propose no new diagnostic. We measure whether the existing ones agree, and which one can
be trusted.

To answer these questions we built WM-Concord, an open benchmark that runs four diagnostic families through one
interface on one controlled zoo of models whose domain is a deterministic finite automaton, so the true state is
known at every step. The zoo varies architecture, scale, training distribution and seed on purpose, so that models
differ in quality and the diagnostics have something to disagree about. Every choice that could be tuned after
seeing results was frozen with a git tag before the results it affects existed.

\textbf{Contributions.} (i) The first head to head comparison of four world model diagnostics on the same models
and the same ground truth. (ii) A test of their predictive validity against a fragility suite frozen before any
diagnostic score. (iii) An open benchmark with a common interface, so a future diagnostic can be judged against
these four on identical ground truth.

\textbf{Findings.} Our headline is a confound. On Othello the four diagnostics agree because they all track
competence, the model's legal move rate; with competence controlled, the diagnostics that can be separated from it
do not measure the same thing (Section~\ref{sec:h1}). Their disagreement is consistent with seed noise
(Section~\ref{sec:h2}). No diagnostic reliably predicts fragility: the representation probe adds to the legal move
rate under the preregistered test, but an exploratory analysis shows that this is a difference between training
distributions, not a link among comparable models (Sections~\ref{sec:h3} and~\ref{sec:explore}). The practical
advice is to report several diagnostics, always with a competence control and within training distributions, rather
than trust any single one.

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews the diagnostics, Section~\ref{sec:data} the domains, Section~\ref{sec:method} the
zoo, the diagnostics and the preregistered analysis, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} what they mean and where they stop.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Representation probing}
Li et al.~\cite{li2023othello} trained a GPT model on Othello move sequences alone. It proposes legal moves almost
every time, probes on its activations recover the board, and editing the decoded board inside the model changes its
predictions to match, which is evidence that the representation is used and not only present. Nanda et
al.~\cite{nanda2023emergent} showed that the board is in fact linear once squares are labelled relative to the
player to move (``mine'' and ``theirs'') rather than by colour. Yuan and S{\o}gaard~\cite{yuan2025revisiting} found
similar board representations in seven language models fine tuned on Othello. These works use probing as their only
instrument. We use it as one of four, with a control task and a causal check, and compare its verdict with the other
three on the same models. Toshniwal et al.~\cite{toshniwal2022chess} tested state tracking in chess behaviourally,
by prompting the model for piece locations; this is close in spirit to our next token validity.

\subsection{State equivalence and inductive bias}
Vafa et al.~\cite{vafa2024evaluating} define the domain as a deterministic finite automaton and derive two metrics
from the Myhill Nerode theorem~\cite{nerode1958linear}: compression error (two histories that reach the same state
are treated differently) and distinction error (two different states are treated the same). On Manhattan taxi
routes, Othello and logic puzzles, models with near perfect next token accuracy failed these metrics, and models
trained on random walks built more coherent maps than models trained on shortest paths. In a second line of
work~\cite{vafa2025inductive}, the same group adapts a pretrained model to small tasks generated from a postulated
world model and asks whether it extrapolates as that world model would; symbolic regression on the adapted models
recovered laws that make no physical sense. These are our D2 and D3. Both works compare their metric only with next
token accuracy; we compare them with each other, with probing, and with an external criterion.

\subsection{Probing confounds}
Hewitt and Liang~\cite{hewitt2019control} showed that high probe accuracy does not show that a model encodes a
property, because an expressive probe can learn the task by itself; they proposed control tasks and selectivity,
the probe's accuracy minus its accuracy on random labels. Belinkov~\cite{belinkov2022probing} reviewed the
method's limits: a probe shows that information can be decoded, not that the model uses it. We report selectivity
for every probe, use a linear probe by default, and record models whose board is decodable but not used as a
separate outcome.

\subsection{Measurement validity and inference}
Raji et al.~\cite{raji2021benchmark} argued that benchmarks are often read as measures of a broad capability they
cannot establish; world model diagnostics face the same risk when a pass on one diagnostic is read as evidence of a
world model. For inference, models trained from the same configuration with different seeds are near copies, so
observations are clustered. Cameron et al.~\cite{cameron2008bootstrap} show that resampling whole clusters gives
honest intervals when clusters are few, and Bouthillier et al.~\cite{bouthillier2021variance} show that seed
variation is often as large as the differences papers report. Every interval in this paper resamples whole
configurations, and disagreement is judged against the spread between seeds.

\section{Domains and Data}
\label{sec:data}

Every domain is defined by a deterministic finite automaton, so the true state is known after every token, and
every domain emits the same triple: the token sequence, the true state, and the set of legal next tokens. Othello is
complete; navigation follows with the same code; a small program interpreter is held out for testing whether the
findings generalize (H4).

\subsection{Othello}
\textbf{The game.} Othello is played on an $8\times8$ board by two players. A move places a disc so that at least
one straight line of the opponent's discs is enclosed between it and another disc of the mover's colour, and every
enclosed disc is flipped. A player with no legal move passes, and the game ends when neither can move, usually after
60 moves.

\textbf{Why Othello.} Three properties make it the standard test bed for world model
studies~\cite{li2023othello, nanda2023emergent}. The state (board and player to move) is exact and small, so every
diagnostic is scored against the truth. The board is hard to read from the moves: the move list says where discs
were placed, never which were flipped, so knowing which discs are \emph{mine} requires replaying the rules, which is
the internal state a world model should track. And the legal moves depend on the whole board, so a model that only
learns which squares tend to follow which cannot stay legal for a whole game.

\textbf{Tokens and data.} As in Othello GPT~\cite{li2023othello}, tokens 1 to 60 are the playable squares and 0 is
padding; passes are not tokens. Our own rules engine replays every game; it is the only copy of the rules, shared by
training, every diagnostic and the tests, and it found no illegal game in 101,000 replayed synthetic games. The
\emph{synthetic} distribution is the \texttt{othello\_world} corpus of Li et al.\ (about 19 million games of random
legal moves). The \emph{championship} distribution is the WTHOR tournament archive: 137,548 human games from 1977 to
2025, which after removing 41 duplicates split into 110,005 training, 13,751 validation and 13,751 test games. The
\emph{mixed} distribution pools both. Every model draws 1,280,000 training games uniformly with replacement from its
pool; the mixed pool holds 640,000 synthetic and the 110,005 championship games, so mixed training is about 85\%
synthetic by games, while its validation and test sets are half of each. The training split is used only for
training, the validation split for checks during training and for fitting diagnostics, and the test split only for
final scores.

\subsection{Navigation}
Navigation follows Vafa et al.~\cite{vafa2024evaluating}: taxi routes on the street graph of Manhattan. A route is
the start intersection, the destination intersection, the turns taken (each one of eight compass directions) and an
end token. All three distributions use one canonical map from OpenStreetMap with 4,579 intersections and 9,705
street segments; the vocabulary has 4,589 tokens, and every route has at most 99 tokens. The state is the current
intersection together with the destination, and the legal next tokens are the turns available there, plus the end
token at the destination. The three training distributions are shortest paths, noisy shortest paths and random
walks, with 2,000,000 training routes each. Validation and test are split by start and destination pair (107,331
held out pairs, half each), so no test pair appears in any training route. The 6.1\% of shortest path routes that
use a street missing from the canonical map are dropped.

\subsection{Held out interpreter}
The interpreter is a small deterministic register and stack machine whose full state is known after every
instruction. Its models are trained and scored only after H1 to H3 are fixed on Othello and navigation.

\subsection{Anchors}
Two published Othello GPTs~\cite{li2023othello}, trained on synthetic and on championship games, and the three
released navigation models of Vafa et al.~\cite{vafa2024evaluating} serve as anchors: every diagnostic scores them
to check our code against published findings, but they never enter H1 to H4.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Model zoo}
The zoo crosses 3 architectures, 3 scales, 3 training distributions and 3 seeds, which gives 81 models per domain.
The architectures are a GPT style transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, a stacked LSTM~\cite{hochreiter1997lstm}
and Mamba~\cite{gu2023mamba} (the plain PyTorch \texttt{mambapy} build). They remember the past in different ways
(attention over the whole history, a fixed size recurrent state, a selective state space recurrence), which is the
axis along which H2 asks whether the diagnostics disagree. The scales are small (4 layers, width 128), medium
(6 layers, width 256) and large (8 layers, width 512), from 0.48 to 25.3 million parameters. Every model gets the
same budget: 5,000 steps of 256 games, AdamW with learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, 200 warmup steps and weight decay
0.01. A model is \emph{admitted} if its legal move rate on 1,000 validation games is at least 0.50 (an untrained
model scores about 0.15); this rate is also the model's \emph{competence} in the analysis. Models that fail are
still scored and reported.

\subsection{How the models see a game}
\label{sec:see}
Every model maps a batch of move sequences to scores for the next move at every position: tokens of shape
$(B, T)$, one integer per move, go through a learned embedding to $(B, T, D)$, through the layers at $(B, T, D)$,
and through a linear head to $(B, T, 61)$, one score per token. $B$ is the batch (256 in training), $T \le 59$ the
positions read and $D \in \{128, 256, 512\}$ the width. The 61 is the vocabulary, not the sequence length: written
one hot, the input is $(B, T, 61)$ and the embedding is a learned $61 \times D$ matrix, which is where one integer
per move becomes $D$ numbers. A batch is 256 whole games drawn from the training pool, each cut to its first 59
tokens and padded with 0; nothing is cut into windows.

Training is next move prediction at every prefix position. For a game $m_1,\dots,m_T$ the model receives
$m_1,\dots,m_{T-1}$ and is trained with
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \log p_\theta\!\left(m_{t+1}\mid m_1,\dots,m_t\right),
\end{equation}
ignoring padded positions. For the first synthetic test game, C4 C5 D6 E3 \dots\ (tokens 27 33 40 21 \dots), the
pairs are C4$\to$C5, C4 C5$\to$D6, C4 C5 D6$\to$E3, up to 58 moves$\to$H5. All 58 predictions are made in one
forward pass and all enter the loss. So the pairs are $[a] \to b$, $[a\,b] \to c$, \dots, not a fixed window
($[a\,b\,c] \to d$, $[b\,c\,d] \to e$): each prediction is sequence to one, made at every position, and the history
only grows. This is teacher forcing: the model always reads the real history, never its own earlier guesses.

An LSTM reads one token per step and carries a memory $(h_t, c_t)$ per layer, so at step $t$ it sees only the new
move but predicts from a summary of the whole history (Fig.~\ref{fig:lstm}). On this game the trained small LSTM
(synthetic, seed 0) reads C4 and predicts C5 ($p = 0.29$, legal), then reads C5 with the memory of C4 and predicts
E6 (legal), then reads D6 and predicts E3 (legal). Running the game one token at a time gives the same top move as
the single training pass at all 58 positions. The transformer has the same input and output shapes but attends to
all earlier positions under a causal mask; Mamba carries a selective state. The diagnostics read these same scores
(D1, D2), adapt the same model (D3) and probe the same $(B, T, D)$ vectors (D4). Navigation uses the same interface
with 4,589 tokens, at most 98 positions read, and a loss on the turns and the end token only.

\subsection{The four diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics}
Every diagnostic implements one interface, \texttt{score(model, domain)}, and writes one score per model, where
higher always means more world model like. Final scores use 1,000 test games of the model's own training
distribution. Protocols were fixed in the preregistration and tuned, if at all, only on the two Othello GPT
anchors, never on a zoo model. Notation: $x_{\le t}$ is the history after $t$ moves, $L(x_{\le t})$ the set of truly
legal next moves, and $f(x_{\le t})$ the model's scores.

\textbf{D1, next token validity.} The share of the $N$ test positions whose top move is legal:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{D1}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{g,t}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\arg\max_v f(x_{g,\le t})_v \in L(x_{g,\le t})\right].
\end{equation}
Nothing is fitted.

\textbf{D2, state equivalence.} Following Myhill Nerode~\cite{nerode1958linear} and Vafa et
al.~\cite{vafa2024evaluating}, two histories are the same state exactly when every continuation is equally allowed
after both; we use the next move as the continuation. The model's accepted set $A(h)$ is its $k$ best moves, with
$k=|L(h)|$, so no threshold is tuned. Over up to 2,000 same state pairs $S$ (different move lists, same board and
player) and 2,000 different state pairs $D$ (same length, different legal sets),
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{CE}&=\operatorname*{mean}_{(h,g)\in S}\frac{|A(h)\,\triangle\,A(g)|}{|A(h)\cup A(g)|},\\
  \mathrm{DE}&=\operatorname*{mean}_{(h,g)\in D}\big(1-\rho(h,g)\big),\\
  \rho(h,g)&=\frac{|B_h\cap A(h)\setminus A(g)|+|B_g\cap A(g)\setminus A(h)|}{|B_h|+|B_g|},
\end{align}
where $B_h=L(h)\setminus L(g)$ and $B_g=L(g)\setminus L(h)$ are the moves legal after only one of the two
histories, so $\rho$ is the share of these boundary moves the model separates correctly, and
$\mathrm{D2}=1-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathrm{CE}+\mathrm{DE})$. Each error has a standard error of at most
0.011.

\textbf{D3, inductive bias probe.} Following~\cite{vafa2025inductive}, a copy of the model with a new linear head is
adapted on only 64 labelled validation positions to four questions ``is square $X$ mine?'' ($X$ = C4, F5, C3, F6),
for 300 AdamW steps (head learning rate $10^{-3}$, body $10^{-4}$). We ask ``mine'' and not ``occupied'' because
occupancy can be read straight off the move list. A model of the same architecture and size with random weights is
adapted the same way, and with balanced accuracy BA on 1,000 test positions,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{D3}=\frac{1}{12}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\sum_{s=0}^{2}\left[\mathrm{BA}(f_{j,s})-\mathrm{BA}(f^{0}_{j,s})\right]
\end{equation}
over 4 tasks and 3 seeds: the help that pretraining gives on board questions. A symbolic regression
arm~\cite{cranmer2023pysr} fits short formulas from the true board to each adapted model's answers and records
whether they recover the ``is $X$ mine'' rule or only an \emph{occupancy shortcut}.

\textbf{D4, representation probe.} After every layer $\ell$, a linear probe $P_\ell$ reads each of the 64 squares
as mine, theirs or empty~\cite{nanda2023emergent}. Following~\cite{hewitt2019control}, an identical control probe
$Q_\ell$ learns fixed random labels that depend only on the square just played and the move number. Probes are fit
on 10,000 validation games, and
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Sel}_\ell=\mathrm{Acc}(P_\ell)-\mathrm{Acc}(Q_\ell),\qquad \mathrm{D4}=\mathrm{Sel}_{\ell^{*}},
\end{equation}
measured on the test games, where $\ell^{*}$ maximizes selectivity on 1,000 further validation games. A \emph{causal check} after
Li et al.~\cite{li2023othello} edits the activations from the middle layer to the last until the probes read one
flipped disc, and compares the share of boundary moves the model then gets right with the same share after a random
edit of equal size. A model with selectivity of at least 0.10 is \emph{decodable}; it is \emph{decodable and causal}
if its causal score is at least 0.20 with an interval above 0, and \emph{decodable but not causal} otherwise. An MLP
probe is reported as a secondary result.

\subsection{Fragility suite}
\label{sec:fragility}
H3 needs an outcome no diagnostic could have been tuned toward. The suite keeps the rules fixed and shifts the
situations, so the right answer always comes from the true rules. With $r_0$ the model's legal top move rate on its
own test games, each variant $v$ gives a drop $\delta_v$: the other training distributions, three board symmetries
(rotation by 180 degrees and the two diagonal reflections, the only symmetries that keep the start position),
positions right after a pass (compared with positions at the same move number and with the same number of legal
moves), and self play with forced detours, where with probability $p\in\{0.1, 0.5\}$ the model's move is replaced by
a random legal move or by the one it rates lowest. Then
\begin{equation}
  F=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v\in V}\delta_v,\qquad \text{relative fragility}=F/r_0,
\end{equation}
so that models of different competence are comparable. Version 1 of the suite was frozen on October 6 (tag
\texttt{fragility-v1}). Before any zoo model was scored, we found that two of its parts could not measure fragility
on this zoo (detour games stopped at the first illegal proposal, and pass positions were compared with all
positions); version 2 (tag \texttt{fragility-v2}, October 18) fixed both. H3 uses relative fragility v2; v1 is the
preregistered secondary outcome.

\subsection{Whole game measures}
\label{sec:seqmethod}
D1, D2, the probes of D4 and most of the fragility suite judge the next move given the real history. This is the
unit every diagnostic in the literature is defined on, but a model can be legal at almost every step and still lose
track of the board over a long game, because under teacher forcing an error never enters the history it reads next.
We therefore add two whole game measures, decided after the Othello results were read and reported as exploratory.
\emph{Legality by move number} splits D1 by the move being predicted. In \emph{free play} the model plays 1,000
games against itself: its top move is checked against the rules at every step, the move actually played is drawn
from its own probabilities over the legal moves, and each game runs to its end or to move 59 (no model is trained to
predict move 60). We report the legal rate, the share of games with no illegal top move (full games) and the move of
the first error. Both measures are preregistered for navigation.

Analyses decided after the preregistered results were read are labelled exploratory and never replace a
preregistered result.

\textbf{Intervals.} The 81 models form 27 configurations of three seeds. Every interval is a 95\% percentile
cluster bootstrap~\cite{cameron2008bootstrap}: each of 2,000 resamples draws 27 configurations with replacement and
keeps all seeds of each.

\textbf{H1, concordance.} For each of the six pairs we compute Kendall's tau b~\cite{kendall1938tau}, and Kendall's
W~\cite{kendall1939w} across all four. A pair \emph{agrees} if tau is at least 0.50 and its interval excludes 0,
\emph{disagrees} if its interval includes 0 or tau is below 0.30, and is \emph{partial} otherwise. Because every
diagnostic may simply follow competence $c$, each pair is also tested with Kendall's partial
tau~\cite{kendall1942partial},
\begin{equation}
  \tau_{xy\cdot c}=\frac{\tau_{xy}-\tau_{xc}\,\tau_{yc}}{\sqrt{(1-\tau_{xc}^{2})(1-\tau_{yc}^{2})}},
\end{equation}
and W is recomputed on ranks with competence regressed out. A pair that agrees raw but not after the control
agrees \emph{through competence}. If tau(D1, competence) is at least 0.90, the controlled D1 pairs are not
interpretable. As a sensitivity check, H1 is repeated on the models with competence of at least 0.90.

\textbf{H2, disagreement.} Each model's disagreement is the standard deviation of its four rank percentiles,
regressed on architecture, scale and training distribution. Disagreement is \emph{systematic} if a factor is
significant at 0.05 after Holm correction and its effect exceeds the seed noise floor, the spread of disagreement
across the seeds of one configuration.

\textbf{H3, predictive validity.} The outcome is relative fragility v2, whose distribution is reported first. D1 is
the reference. For D2, D3 and D4 we compute the partial Spearman correlation with the outcome given D1, and a least
squares regression of the outcome on D1, the diagnostic, architecture and scale. A diagnostic \emph{adds to D1} if
its partial correlation interval excludes 0, and is the \emph{best predictor} if it is the strongest and the
interval of each difference to the other two excludes 0.

\textbf{H4, generalization.} H1 to H3 are fixed on Othello and navigation before any interpreter model is scored;
the findings generalize if every pair keeps its H1 category and the H3 verdict is the same on the interpreter.

\subsection{Navigation protocol}
\label{sec:navmethod}
Navigation runs the same zoo, diagnostics and analysis, with the changes fixed in Amendment 5 (tag
\texttt{prereg-v1.5}) before any navigation model was trained. Admission and D1 are as for Othello on routes (an
untrained model scores about 0.0005). D2 uses the state (current intersection, destination). D3 asks three questions
about the state at a random position: is the destination at most 10 turns away, at most 823.9 m away, and is the
current intersection east of it (the medians of the data, with a fourth task dropped by a rule written before its
numbers were computed because a shortcut answered it). D4 probes the current intersection (4,579 classes), with a
control task over (token just read, turns so far), and the causal check moves the probe's reading to a neighbouring
intersection with a different legal set. The fragility suite \texttt{maps-fragility-v1} has the other distributions,
random and adversarial detours on drives the model makes itself, and the least visited intersections; no symmetry
maps the street graph onto itself. Free play and full route rates are a preregistered secondary measure. Because the
Othello result came from a training distribution difference, navigation adds, as a preregistered secondary analysis,
the H3 regression with training distribution as a covariate and the partial correlation within each distribution.
Models are trained and scored by architecture (all transformers, then LSTMs, then Mamba), and on the cutoff of
November 22, 2026 the analysis runs on the architectures whose 27 models are all scored.

\section{Experimental Setup}
Training and scoring ran on four AWS g5.2xlarge instances (one NVIDIA A10G with 24 GB each), one per author, each
with a slice of the zoo. Training one Othello model at the full budget takes 1.5, 4.7 and 16.1 minutes for the
small, medium and large transformer, 0.6 to 5.9 minutes for the LSTMs and 17.7 to 145.7 minutes for Mamba in plain
PyTorch, about 37 GPU hours per domain. Every training record stores the checkpoint's SHA 256 hash, which every
later step checks; every result records the commit that produced it; library versions are pinned; and the test
suite runs in GitHub Actions on every pull request.

\subsection{Exploratory: next move versus whole game}
\label{sec:seqres}
The teacher forced rate reproduces every model's D1 exactly, and in free play each model played at least 978
distinct games of 1,000 (Table~\ref{tab:sequence}, Fig.~\ref{fig:sequence}). Three findings stand out.

First, playing its own games is much harder than following real ones for models trained on human games. Free play is
below the teacher forced rate by 0.03 for synthetic and 0.014 for mixed training, but by 0.24 for championship
training, and lower for all 27 championship models: they are legal 81\% of the time on human test games but 58\% in
their own games, with the first illegal move at move 7 on average. As soon as the random opening takes them off
human lines they fail, the same weakness the fragility suite found.

Second, the LSTMs lose track of the board over a long game. With the real history their legal rate is flat after the
opening, but in their own games it falls steadily (for the large LSTM, from 0.74 at move 10 to 0.64 at move 30 and
0.43 at move 58), while transformers and Mamba stay flat, so D1 understates how much worse LSTMs are at playing a
whole game.

Third, a whole game without an illegal move is rare: only the large transformers trained on synthetic or mixed games
and the large Mamba models trained on synthetic games reach it in more than 10\% of games (at most 0.219 in the zoo,
against 0.965 for the synthetic Othello GPT), and 40 of 81 models never manage one.

The free play legal rate ranks models much as D1 does (Spearman 0.891 [0.730, 0.951] with D1, 0.884 [0.724, 0.946]
with competence) and is not related to relative fragility ($-0.173$ [$-0.594$, 0.371]). With D1 controlled, D4 is
the only diagnostic that relates to the full game rate (partial Spearman 0.324 [0.136, 0.512]; D2 0.064, D3
$-0.001$, both intervals including 0), which fits the causal check: models whose board can be read and used play
cleaner whole games. Whole game measures do not change the H1 to H3 answers, but they show two failures the next
move unit misses.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Why published verdicts disagree.} Published claims for and against world models in Othello rely on
different diagnostics, and our results suggest how they can disagree without either being wrong in its own terms:
the diagnostics share a large common factor, competence, and beyond it they measure different things. A study that
reports one diagnostic on models of different quality will mostly be reporting how good the models are.

\textbf{What each diagnostic licenses.} D1 measures competence and little more. D2 adds whether predictions depend
on the board rather than the move order, but its distinction error shows that even good models often fail to
separate boards with different legal moves. D3, at our preregistered protocol, gives small gaps on the zoo and
partly rewards an occupancy shortcut that the move list gives away. D4 shows that the board is decodable in every
model, while the causal check shows that only a few models, almost all trained on synthetic games, use it.
``Decodable'' is therefore not the same claim as ``has a world model'', as Li et al.~\cite{li2023othello} and
Belinkov~\cite{belinkov2022probing} warned.

\textbf{Predictive validity needs comparable models.} On Othello the fragility suite mostly separates the
championship trained models from the rest, so any diagnostic that also separates them looks predictive. The
preregistered test was passed by D4, but the link disappears within each training distribution. Evaluations of
world model diagnostics should therefore report predictive validity within training distributions, not only across
them; we preregistered exactly this control for navigation before any navigation score existed.

\textbf{Recommendations.} Report several diagnostics rather than one; control for competence before reading
agreement as shared meaning; compare models within a training distribution before claiming that a diagnostic
predicts robustness; pair probes with a control task and a causal check; and add a whole game measure, because the
next move unit hides both drift over long sequences and failure off the training distribution.

\textbf{Limitations.} The 81 models are 27 configurations, so intervals are honest but wide, and within distribution
analyses rest on nine configurations each. D2 uses next move continuations only, a simplification of Vafa et
al.~\cite{vafa2024evaluating}, and its same board pairs come mostly from the opening. D3 measures help from
pretraining with 64 examples, not closeness to a perfect answer. Fragility is small for most models, so H3 rests on
the minority of fragile ones. The undisturbed self play run that the fragility detours are compared with plays the
model's best legal move, so its games collapse into at most four distinct games; we found this after scoring, kept
the preregistered suite unchanged, and the navigation suite draws its undisturbed games from the model's own
probabilities. The mixed distribution trains on about 85\% synthetic games. In training a model reads at most 58
tokens, so no model is trained to predict move 60. All models share a modest budget of 5,000 steps, the LSTMs are
the weakest family under it, and Mamba runs without its optimized kernels; the best zoo models remain below the
published Othello GPT. Finally, the whole game measures were added after the Othello results were read and are
exploratory here, though preregistered for navigation.

\section{Conclusion}
On Othello, the four world model diagnostics agree mainly because they all track competence, none of them reliably
predicts when a model will break, and the one link to fragility comes from the training data. In detail, on 81
models and under a preregistered analysis: the diagnostics agree on which models are better, but almost entirely
through competence (H1); their remaining disagreement is not systematic in architecture, scale or training data
(H2); and no single diagnostic wins, with the representation probe's added value traced to differences between
training distributions (H3). The same pipeline, with its rules frozen in advance, runs on navigation, where our code
already reproduces the published results, and the held out interpreter will test whether these findings generalize
(H4). Any new world model diagnostic can be added through the common interface and judged against these four on
identical ground truth.
